I visited Japan years ago when I was first studying the language, and it was a magnificent opportunity. I met many amazing people on my trip who taught me amazing things about the world and myself. It's strange to think how much time has already passed since then.

However, when I look back at the pictures I took then, knowing even more that I do now about Japan's culture, it's truly surreal how much they were really showing me, and as a westerner I did not have the awareness to realize it.

When my second host family took me and my mother to an iris farm just outside their prefecture, I was enthralled by the flowers I saw there; the irises were all healthy and soaking up the summer sun and heat. The waters were clean and nourishing, and simply being at the waters' edge was cool and refreshing.

![Image](https://dl.dropbox.com/scl/fi/8thoznb6d7iw6mm75tw4o/100_0645.JPG?rlkey=whey8e22odg7xoxn9m0yb55in&st=cqb1v882&dl=0)

As a young artist I was already drawing all sorts of subjects and objects from life. Not only did I not know how important this small outing would be to my development as a photographer and creator in general, but I was unaware that I was being led to see something deeper about Japan; shown a secret gift to appreciate for years to come.&nbsp;

I remember taking so many pictures of these *ayame*, or iris flowers, which were scattered across these marsh fields in a beautiful array of colors and a feast for the eyes. However, could I have been more present? Should I have been more inclined to these plant beings spirits, acknowledging the peace they brought to my soul and noticing their humble yet radiant nature?&nbsp;

The tradition of *Hanami* or flower viewing in Japan is a practice steeped in culture, dating back to the Nara period in the 8th century. Usually it's *sakura* blossoms, or cherry blossoms that reign in attracting utmost attention from onlookers and those who seek flowering beauty throughout the seasons, however, today I am humbly surprised to know that iris flowers count also in this glorious tradition.

![Image](https://dl.dropbox.com/scl/fi/ln3mocmn69sfxd2k8d5hv/100_0640.JPG?rlkey=wco1dntpjnlf0kyfs7dzktn0f&st=9pc9612t&dl=0)

My soul was heightened in that garden; looking at and taking photographs of those irises elevated me in ways I could not even begin to describe. However, I would not know how much until years later. Irises have special meaning to me: I painted an iris flower and mailed it to a voice acting friend so that she could finally get her work visa. Growing up our gardens were filled with irises. I drew, and painted many irises. Their structure and scent were intoxicating to me.&nbsp;

When someone shows you their culture; the core of their traditions, they are showing you one of the deepest parts of themselves and their part of the world. This is what I've come to realize, and what I wished I knew back then.&nbsp;

What did this teach me about my artwork? The brain takes time to incorporate knowledge into its repertoire of tools, ergo it took many years of connecting the dots for me to integrate all of these important realizations. They taught me that it's okay to slow down, that it's also fine to do art at one's own pace and to not churn out pieces constantly. Embedded in this long-term tutelage was a lesson in patience, self-worth, and dedication to my craft which I am honored to commit.&nbsp;
